**The cramped feeling comes from framing and repetition more than from too much data.** I’d keep the list → details → logs workflow, remove most borders, and give selection and failures a clearer hierarchy.

Here’s what is making it busy, in priority order:

| Observation | Why it hurts | What I’d change |
|---|---|---|
| **Three border layers** surround the list and details: app frame → pane frame → inner box. | Your eye crosses several boundaries before reaching five rows of data. | Remove the app frame and both inner boxes. Use whitespace between sections, with at most one divider. |
| **`▶` appears on all five rows.** | It cannot identify selection; it also suggests every row is expandable. | Use reverse video for the selected row only. Reserve disclosure markers for actual expandable groups. |
| **`PASSED` has two adjacent checkmarks**, plus `[PASS]` in the list. | The detail field alone encodes success three times. Meanwhile, the failure gets no stronger visual priority. | Use one status word, optionally reinforced by semantic color. Keep successful rows quiet; emphasize failure. |
| **The selected item and focused pane are unclear.** | Users must guess which job the details and logs describe, and what arrows will control. | Show one selected row, bold the focused section title, and explicitly label the log source. |
| **Logs repeat `[INFO]`, a full date, `::`, and the job name.** | Boilerplate dominates short messages. The first line spends roughly two-thirds of its characters on metadata and delimiters. | Put job/date context in the log heading; show time and message per line. Retain severity for mixed logs, but dim routine INFO. |
| **The footer gives every action equal weight.** | Brackets and long labels compete with the content. “Enter Select” also leaves the result unclear. | Use contextual hints such as `enter logs`, `tab pane`, and `? help`. Move the version into help/about. |

There’s substantial empty space inside the details box while the logs get only three lines. That’s a useful clue: **you have room, but too much of it is allocated to containers.** On a typical list row, six cells are vertical border strokes alone, before padding.

I’d use a full-screen monitoring session with a persistent list and preview at wide sizes. The main loop is: scan statuses → select a job → inspect its output. Navigation, opening logs, switching focus, searching, following output, refreshing, and quitting should support that loop.

This is schematic for a 120-column terminal; let the log region take the remaining height. The `>` stands in for reverse-video selection in the sketch—don’t add it to every row. If branch and commit are shared by every job, keep them in the header and remove them from the preview.

For styling, keep ordinary text in the terminal’s default foreground, timestamps and labels secondary, and failures red **with the word “Failed” still visible**. `Running` and `Waiting` are clearer than `[....]` and `[WAIT]`. Status words also avoid emoji width complications.

Make the smaller layouts deliberate:

- **120+ columns:** list and preview side by side, selected-job logs below.
- **80×24:** full-width job list; Enter opens the selected job with compact metadata above its logs. Esc returns, preserving selection and scroll position.
- **60 columns:** the same single-pane drill-down. Hide secondary columns first, truncate long names within rows, and expose full values in details.

In Bubble Tea/Lipgloss, the practical changes are small: remove nested border styles, derive pane sizes from `tea.WindowSizeMsg`, and measure terminal cells rather than `len(string)`. Keep selection tied to a stable job ID across refreshes. In logs, scrolling upward should pause follow mode visibly; `G` can resume it. Show refresh failures as `Disconnected · last updated …` so a quiet screen cannot be mistaken for a healthy pipeline.

**Start with the borders and repeated arrows.** Those two changes will make the largest immediate difference without changing the information you expose.
